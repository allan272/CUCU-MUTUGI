Feeding Programme
Day-old chicks must be handled with great care. A flock that gets off to a good start is easier to manage and perform well throughout the rearing period. Such a flock will have a higher initial body weight, better uniformity, improved health status, and a greater ability to reach its genetic potential.
Before the chicks arrive, ensure that all equipment and facilities in the house are functioning properly. Check that:
The house temperature is at the recommended level.
Adequate fresh air is available through proper ventilation.
Feed and clean drinking water are readily accessible.
Sufficient lighting is provided to encourage feeding and drinking.
Proper preparation before chick placement is essential for achieving optimal growth and performance.
Rearing and Feeding
Feed represents the largest cost in commercial poultry production. It is therefore essential to purchase feed from a reputable manufacturer who can guarantee consistent quality and performance.
Farmers should avoid mixing feeds from different manufacturers or adding supplementary protein sources such as fish meal, as well as mineral supplements such as dicalcium phosphate (DCP), unless advised by a qualified nutritionist. Such practices can alter the nutrient balance of the feed and negatively affect bird performance. Excessive inclusion of certain ingredients may also reduce product quality. For example, the use of fish meal above the recommended maximum level of 5% can result in an undesirable fishy taste in eggs and meat.
When starting a flock, feeder lids or plastic feeder trays should be provided at a rate of one tray per 100 chicks. Feed may also be spread on clean paper placed over the litter to encourage feed intake during the first few days. As the chicks grow and become accustomed to feeding, gradually remove the trays or feeder lids and replace them with standard adult feeders. By the time the birds are ten (10) days old, all feeder lids and trays should be removed and replaced with standard feeders. Providing adequate feeder space is essential for healthy growth, uniform development, and optimal feed intake. Ensure that the recommended feeder space is maintained at all stages of production.
Feeding Programmes
Different classes of poultry require specific feeds to meet their nutritional requirements at various stages of growth and production.
Broilers
Starter Crumbs or Mash: 0–21 days
Finisher Pellets or Mash: 22–35 days
Layers
Chick and Duckling Mash: Week 1–8
Grower Mash: Week 9–18 (up to point of lay)
Layer Mash: From 18 weeks onwards and throughout the laying period
Improved Indigenous Chickens (Sasso, KARI Improved Kienyeji, Rainbow Rooster, Kuroiler, Kenbro)
Starter Mash: 0–50 days
Grower Mash: 51–75 days
Finisher Mash: 76–90 days (for meat production)
Layer Mash: From 20–25 weeks onwards (for egg production)
Regular monitoring of feed intake and bird performance is important to ensure that the feeding programme meets the nutritional needs of the flock and supports optimum growth, productivity, and profitability.
Water Management
Drinking equipment should be distributed evenly throughout the poultry house and alternated with feeders to ensure easy access for all birds. No bird should have to travel more than 1.5 metres to reach either feed or water. During the first week, provide one chick drinker (fount) for every 75 chicks. As the birds grow, gradually replace chick drinkers with standard drinkers while ensuring adequate drinking space is maintained.
Chick drinkers should be washed and disinfected daily, then refilled with clean, fresh water. Birds must have continuous access to wholesome drinking water, and drinkers should never be allowed to run dry. During hot weather, providing cool drinking water helps maintain feed intake, bird comfort, and overall productivity. Water storage tanks should therefore be protected from direct sunlight and, where possible, have reflective surfaces to minimize water heating.
As birds grow, regularly adjust the height of both drinkers and feeders so that they remain slightly above the level of the birds’ backs. This helps reduce feed and water wastage through spillage.
To maintain water quality and reduce the risk of disease, use a reliable water sanitiser, such as chlorine, to control harmful microorganisms in the drinking water system.
